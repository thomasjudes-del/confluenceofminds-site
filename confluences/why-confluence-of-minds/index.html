Why Confluence of Minds?

An Indian poet and a Scottish polymath exchanged ideas about education, ecology and how knowledge should meet the world. A century later, their conversation still feels strangely current.

Thomas Judes2026-08-085 min read

The name Confluence of Minds did not begin as a branding exercise. It echoes a real encounter between two people who had almost every reason to think differently — and chose to keep talking.

Rabindranath Tagore was a poet, educator and Nobel laureate from Bengal. Patrick Geddes was a Scottish biologist, sociologist and pioneering town planner. They met while Geddes was working in India and exchanged letters from 1918 into 1930.

The subjects moved freely: education, science, the environment, rural reconstruction, cities, universities and the relationship between local life and global knowledge. That freedom matters. Neither man seemed particularly interested in respecting the borders between disciplines simply because institutions had drawn them there.

They did not think alike. That was the point.

Geddes liked systems, surveys, diagrams and plans. Tagore was more suspicious of anything that made living things too tidy. He wanted education to remain connected to nature, intuition, culture and the inner development of the student. Their correspondence is interesting precisely because it is not a story of two identical thinkers discovering that they agree.

It is a story of difference producing a third space.

A confluence is not a blend. Two rivers keep their origins — but create a new current when they meet.Confluence of Minds

In 2017, Bashabi Fraser, Tapati Mukherjee and Amrit Sen edited a collection of their writings under a title that captures this beautifully: A Confluence of Minds: The Rabindranath Tagore and Patrick Geddes Reader on Education and Environment.

Why the phrase still works

Our own Confluence of Minds is not an academic continuation of Tagore and Geddes. It is a small creative echo of the same instinct: put things together that normally live in separate folders and see what happens.

A science-fiction decision game about Titan can sit beside a psychological novel about algorithmic authorship. A sound-only film can coexist with Japanese cultural exploration, fantasy, climate fiction and experiments made with artificial intelligence. The projects do not need a common visual style or even a common medium. The connection is the collision itself.

That is also why the plural matters. Not one mind made larger, but several modes of thought allowed to interfere with one another: scientific and fictional, human and machine, serious and playful, archival and speculative.

Tagore and Geddes were thinking about education and environment, not interactive fiction or generative AI. But their exchange carries a useful warning for the present: specialization gives us depth; confluence gives us unexpected questions.
